## What CBAM is

From 2026, importers of steel, cement, aluminium, fertilisers, hydrogen and electricity pay for the carbon embedded in what they bring into the EU, at the EU ETS price, while the free allowances the same sectors receive inside the EU are phased out. The aim is to stop carbon leakage without giving up the carbon price.

## What we did

We assessed CBAM against the energy trilemma (decarbonisation, affordability, security of supply), extended by two effects the trilemma can't see: export competitiveness and international justice. For each pair of goals we traced the trade-off CBAM creates, then asked whether it meets four tests: a well-functioning economy, less environmental degradation, citizens' welfare and justice, and national security. The paper includes a worked cost calculation for a covered import.

## What we found

CBAM serves the EU economy well in design but unevenly in practice. It protects the integrity of EU climate policy, while its global effect is still uncertain. It matters for national security more than its designers say, but only if export exposure and energy costs are also handled. And it fails international justice where exporters lack the capacity to respond.

Four fixes follow: support and revenue recycling for low-capacity exporters, widening the carbon club, downstream coverage to close the leakage loop, and a safeguard for strategic inputs. The carbon price becomes harder to escape, but it is not yet high enough, verification is the weakest link, and predictability depends on the EU holding to its own schedule.
