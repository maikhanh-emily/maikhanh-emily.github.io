This term a classmate and I wrote [a paper on CBAM](/projects/cbam-energy-trilemma/), the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism. It doesn't mention Vietnam at all. This post is what I wanted to add as a Vietnamese. The views on Vietnam here are my own, not the paper's.

## CBAM, briefly

Since 1 January 2026, companies importing steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, hydrogen and electricity into the EU pay for the carbon emitted in making those goods, at the EU's carbon price. If the producer measures and verifies its real emissions, the bill is based on the real number. If it can't, the default value applies, and that is usually much higher.

## At first, I thought it was fair

When I first heard about CBAM, I thought it was a good rule that creates fairness: goods made inside the EU and goods coming in face the same carbon price. The deeper I looked, the more I saw that if you're not careful, it's easy to end up somewhere unfair.

The example I kept coming back to is carbon credits. Earlier this year the EU adopted a target of cutting emissions 90% by 2040, of which up to 5 percentage points can come from international carbon credits, starting in 2036. Those credits can be bought from countries like Vietnam, where cutting emissions is much cheaper.

Put the two side by side. When Vietnam sells steel into the EU, its carbon is charged at the EU's price. When the EU buys emission cuts from Vietnam, it buys that carbon cheaper. And if Vietnam sells off its cheapest cuts, the ones it has left to make for its own targets will cost more.

## So, is CBAM fair to Vietnam?

Honestly, it's hard to say. Maybe yes, because the rule applies to every country. But if Vietnam isn't careful, doesn't understand the rules well, and doesn't have proper methods to measure, then no.

## Where Vietnam stands

Some countries can already measure, verify and price their carbon. Others can't measure it yet. In my view, Vietnam is still closer to the second group.

The domestic carbon market is young. Vietnam's carbon exchange only opened on the Hanoi Stock Exchange at the end of June 2026 and is still a pilot, with emission quotas for thermal power, steel and cement handed out for free. Full operation only starts in 2029.

And steel is Vietnam's largest export in CBAM's scope. In 2023, Vietnam sold more than 2.5 million tonnes of steel to the EU, about a quarter of all its steel exports.

## What I saw at FPT IS

When I worked in green transformation consulting at FPT IS, only Vietnam's large companies were starting to pay attention to their carbon numbers. In general, people didn't care much yet.

Under CBAM, if you can't measure, the default value applies. The EU buyer pays the difference, and that gives them a reason to choose another supplier.

## My answer

So my answer is still: hard to say. But most of the answer sits on Vietnam's side. Do we understand the rules? Can we measure our own emissions? And are we careful enough with deals that look like a good idea?
