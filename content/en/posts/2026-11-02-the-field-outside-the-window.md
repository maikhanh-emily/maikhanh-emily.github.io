I had never left Vietnam before. On my first day in Germany, on the train from Frankfurt Airport to Nuremberg, I saw a vast field of solar panels. I grew up in Hanoi, and nothing like that exists there. I couldn't stop thinking about it for the rest of the ride.

I know how that sounds. A view from a train window isn't usually where a career comes from. So here is what actually changed, checked against the record.

## A subject, then a building site

In September 2023 I left Foreign Trade University in Hanoi for a semester at Friedrich-Alexander-Universität Erlangen-Nürnberg, with a focus on sustainable business practices. So sustainability was already on my timetable. But it was a subject: something to read, discuss and be examined on.

Germany was treating it as a building site. That April it had closed its last three nuclear plants. By the end of the year, renewables covered more than half of its electricity use for the first time.

Home had looked different that summer. From late May to mid-June, a heatwave and low reservoirs left northern Vietnam short of power, and there were rolling cuts in Hanoi, where I was doing an internship. I had gone from a city that had just run short of electricity to a country rebuilding its power system in plain sight.

## The question it left me with

What stayed with me was less the panels than what they implied. The technology for the energy transition already exists. What decides whether a green project gets built or stays on paper is almost always the financial case.

Vietnam, I learned later, shows both halves of that. It does have solar fields, mostly on the south-central coast, a long way from Hanoi. A generous feed-in tariff brought in around 9 GW in 2020 alone. The tariff closed at the end of that year, and the grid in those provinces couldn't carry everything that had been built. The engineering wasn't the problem. The tariff and the grid decided what happened.

## Checking the claim

I'm suspicious of stories in which one moment changes a life, so I went through my own CV. Before that semester: a paper on a trade agreement and fishery exports, tax compliance dashboards, sponsorship pitches for a university sports club. All useful, none of it about energy or climate. After it:

- **2024.** The [investment case for an offshore wind project in Vietnam](/projects/gefe-offshore-wind/). It didn't turn on the turbines. It turned on what it would cost to connect to the grid, when the rules would settle, and what the project meant for coastal communities.
- **2024–25.** [Sustainability reporting at FPT IS](/projects/fpt-carbon-accounting/). The problem wasn't that cutting emissions was technically hard. It was that companies couldn't produce numbers anyone could rely on.
- **2025.** A [circular food model](/projects/one-earth-next-generation-leaders/), with a team of six from six countries, in Hong Kong.
- **2025.** A master's in finance at BI in Norway. That question is what took me there.
- **2026.** [Business development at Planck](/projects/planck-bd-crm/), a start-up designing materials for CO₂ capture. The science is new. Whether it reaches industrial scale will be decided commercially, not in the lab.

So the claim holds up. I went to Germany for a semester, came back with a direction, and two years later moved to Norway to follow it.

## Why energy

Somewhere on that list the broad word narrowed. Sustainability was the way in. Energy is where I want to stay, because that is where the largest capital decisions of the next twenty years will be made, and the commercial side is where those decisions get made or blocked. It's also why I study finance and not engineering.

Germany has come back too. My [master's thesis](/projects/thesis-climate-risk-germany/), written with a classmate, builds a daily index of climate-risk attention from ten years of German news and tests what it does to German share prices. My German is still basic. The topic models don't mind.

I never found out exactly where that field was, or who paid for it. These days, the second question is the one I'd ask first.
