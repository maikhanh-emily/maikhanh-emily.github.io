In five months at a deep-tech start-up I submitted more than 20 funding applications: public funds, prizes, pitch competitions, accelerators and corporate programmes. Some were a few pages long, one was over 40. I won't name programmes or results here. This is what I took from them.

## The one I remember most

It was a resubmission to a large European programme. Before I joined, the company had applied to it three or four times, and it hadn't got through once. This time the file was handed to me.

I didn't start by writing. First I set up a meeting with someone from the programme, to hear how they saw things from their side. After that conversation I understood the programme much better.

Then I took the lead and carried the whole application: 46 pages, not counting the supporting documents. The technical team helped with the technology and the specialist parts, of course, but most of it I did myself, building on the earlier submissions, what I'd learned from the programme's side and input from my technical colleagues.

## Before and after

Before I started, I thought writing applications was about being precise. Precise about each programme's goals and about who reads it: are they experts in the field or not, and so how much do I need to explain? Precise in the wording, in every detail, and in the supporting documents too.

After more than 20, I still think so.

I'd applied for competitions and scholarships before, so I already knew the most important thing is fit: whether we really match what the programme is looking for. That's why the first question I asked, every time, was whether a programme was worth applying to at all.

## One story, told differently

A company has one story, but every programme has a different goal and a different reader, so the telling has to change.

For an expert panel I had to go deep, use the right technical terms and be exact. For non-experts I had to find a way to explain it simply without it becoming wrong. With deep tech, that is harder than it sounds.

## The part nobody sees

Understanding each programme: what they're really after, who will read it, and from there which details to put in and in what order.

A detail that fits programme A perfectly won't necessarily work if you carry it unchanged into programme B. So every application meant choosing again, even though the story underneath stayed the same.

## On rejection

Not every application got through, and I'm comfortable with a "no". I believe rejection leads to redirection. And sometimes it isn't even your fault. It's just the wrong timing and a bit of bad luck.

After more than 20 of them, I at least know how to put an application together so that the reader doesn't overlook the potential behind it.
